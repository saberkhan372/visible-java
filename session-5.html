Meeting 05 · Sunday, December 13 · 7–9 a.m. PT / 10 a.m.–noon ET

Classroom studio

No new Java today. You bring one lesson package from any session, try it on colleagues the way your students would meet it, get structured feedback, and revise. You leave with one lesson you are ready to teach. Two optional extensions, AI-generated code and hardware sensors, are saved for the last half hour.

Java ideas
Nothing new. Whatever your lesson uses from Sessions 1–4.
AP link
Optional: tie your lesson to an AP CS A unit and an exam skill, such as tracing code, writing a method, writing a class, or testing. Outside AP: the critique protocol works for any lesson in any subject.
Files
session-5-files.zip: a blank student-handout template, a studio guide with the package checklist and critique protocol, and a sensor stand-in sketch for the hardware extension.
You leave with
One classroom-ready lesson package, revised after colleague feedback, with a plan for the first day you teach it.

Can’t make it live? The studio works with one colleague and an hour. Follow On your own. Didn’t finish a package? Bring a draft, or bring the handout from any session unedited. Half-finished work is exactly what the studio is for.

0:00 · 10 minutes · Predict and discuss

Predict your own lesson

Open the lesson package you brought. Before anyone looks at it, write your answers to the two questions. You will check them against what your colleagues actually do at 0:55.

  1. Where will your students get stuck first? Name the line, the question, or the minute.

    “Question 4 of the prediction table” A strong answer is specific enough to check. “They’ll find it hard” can’t be wrong, so it can’t teach you anything. “They’ll write 10 for frame 5” can.

  2. What will a student who understands write that a student who doesn’t won’t?

    “The value that made the condition true” For the Session 2 lab: a student who understands names 390, or any number above 380. A student who patched the bug says “I changed && to ||.” If you can’t name the difference, the lesson doesn’t collect it yet.

Facilitator notes

People who didn’t finish a package will feel behind. Say out loud that drafts are the point. Anyone with nothing can take any session’s handout as it is and adapt it today.

Collect a few answers to question 1 in the chat. They are the predictions this meeting tests.

0:10 · 15 minutes · Short demo

The critique protocol, on a lesson everyone knows

The facilitator presents Session 1’s student handout, and two volunteers critique it with the protocol everyone will use at 1:10. The protocol is in the studio guide in the download. Write your answers to questions 3–4 first.

  1. Context, 2 minutes

    Who the students are and where the lesson lands. No apologies or disclaimers.

  2. Try it, 3 minutes

    Colleagues do the first three minutes as students would, out loud.

  3. Clarifying questions, 1 minute

    Questions with short factual answers only.

  4. Warm feedback, 2 minutes

    “What will work for students is…”

  5. Cool feedback, 2 minutes

    “I wonder where a student would get stuck when…” Point to a line, a question, or a minute.

  6. One change, 2 minutes

    The presenter names one change, and makes it before the meeting ends.

  1. Which question in Session 1’s handout will students get wrong most often?

    frame 5 of the prediction table Most students predict that frame 5 draws at 10. It draws at 8, because the circle is drawn before y changes. The Session 1 teacher guide lists it first among the likely misconceptions.

  2. Which part of that handout gives the strongest evidence that a student understands?

    Explain, question 3 “Why does the ball stop moving if float y = 0; is moved inside draw()?” A student can copy a working sketch. A student can’t copy an explanation of scope without understanding it.

Facilitator notes

Model the hardest rule: during warm and cool feedback, the presenter only listens and takes notes. Don’t defend the handout, even when the feedback is wrong. Say so afterward, so everyone sees it is allowed.

Pick a cool-feedback point you genuinely agree with, and make the change on screen. It shows that “one change” means a small, real edit, not a rewrite.

0:25 · 25 minutes · Guided build

Revise your package

Work in your own package. Use the checklist in session-5-teacher-guide.md. If you are starting from nothing, copy session-5-student-handout.md, a blank handout in the same shape as Sessions 1–4.

Supported

Mission 1

Directions a student can follow alone

Give the handout a time, a “you need” line, and steps a student could follow with no teacher talking.

Expect  A colleague can read step 1 and start without asking you anything.

Mission 2

A prediction before any Run

Make sure students commit to an answer in writing before the computer shows them: a table, a trace, or a question with one right answer.

Expect  The prediction has an answer you could check, and it appears before the first “press Run.”

Mission 3

Evidence and one criterion

Add “What to hand in” to the handout. In your teacher notes, write the expected answers and one assessment criterion that looks at reasoning, not at the finished sketch.

Expect  You could grade a stack of these in ten minutes, and the grade would tell you who understood.

Independent extension

Extension 1

A way back in

Make a checkpoint file: the project as it should be halfway through. Say in the handout where a student who falls behind should open it.

Expect  A student who missed the first half can start the second half on time. Every session in this workshop has one for the same reason.

Extension 2

An absent-student route

Write a short “on your own” route, modeled on the ones on these session pages: the steps, the time for each, and where the answers are.

Expect  A student who was out sick can do the lesson at home and hand in the same evidence.

Facilitator notes

Circulate and ask one question per person: “What will a student write in your prediction step?” If they can’t answer, mission 2 isn’t done.

Experienced teachers often want to add more content. Steer them toward the extensions instead, which make the lesson more usable rather than longer.

0:55 · 15 minutes · Classroom adaptation

Teach the first three minutes

In groups of three. Each person teaches the first three minutes of their lesson while the other two act as students: they follow the handout literally and say aloud what they are thinking. One person keeps time. Then rotate.

  1. Teacher, 3 minutes

    Teach as you would in class. Don’t explain the lesson. Teach it.

  2. Students, the whole time

    Do exactly what the handout says, nothing more. When it is unclear, say so out loud instead of guessing.

  3. Teacher, after

    Check your answer to question 1. Did they get stuck where you predicted?

Facilitator notes

Three minutes is short on purpose. The opening of a lesson is where most of the confusion starts, and it is the part teachers rehearse least.

Remind “students” to be literal, not difficult. The goal is to find the sentence the handout is missing, not to trip the teacher up.

1:10 · 15 minutes · Colleague exchange

Structured critique

Same groups of three. Run the protocol from the demo, with warm feedback, cool feedback, and one change, on as many packages as time allows. The presenter listens and doesn’t respond until step 6.

Discussion prompt. Warm: what in this lesson will work for students? Cool: where would a student get stuck, and what is the smallest change that would help?

“Warm: the prediction table comes before any Run, and the frame-5 question is going to start an argument, which is exactly what you want. Cool: I wonder whether a student will know what ‘drew at y’ means in the second column. When I was the student, I had to look at the code to find out. Smallest change: one example row filled in, maybe frame 0.”

Facilitator notes

Visit each group once, and only to keep time and protect the listening rule. Cool feedback that names a specific line is the goal. Redirect “it’s a lot” to “which part could go?”

The sentences groups posted to the chat in Sessions 1–4 are good openers here if a group stalls.

1:25 · 5 minutes · Exit evidence and next step

Before you go

  1. Exit evidence

    Share your package where the cohort shares work, with one sentence: the one change you made today, and why.

  2. Next step

    Teach it. Afterward, add one line to your teacher notes: what students actually did at the step you predicted would be hardest. Every session page and download stays up after the camp.

1:30 · 30 minutes · Optional studio

Studio

Two optional extensions, for anyone who wants them. Neither is required for any lesson in this workshop, and every lesson works without them.

Optional · AI

AI-generated code as a prediction task

Ask an AI assistant for a variation of your starter sketch, such as “make the ball speed up each time it hits a wall.” Don’t run it yet. Students read it, predict what it will do, then run it and find one way the code differs from the request.

Expect  A prediction task you didn’t have to write, and a habit students need: read and test generated code before trusting it. The studio guide has the full routine.

Optional · hardware

A sensor as input

Open SensorStandIn. The ball’s speed comes from readSensor(), which reports 0 to 1023, the range of an Arduino analog pin. For now, the mouse stands in for the sensor.

Expect  With the mouse at the right edge, the corner reads sensor: 1023 and the ball slides right until it stops at the wall. At the left edge it reads 0 and slides left. To use a real sensor, replace only the body of readSensor().

AP-style multiple choice · one last trace

What is printed?

int[] xs = {370, 25, 200, 395};
int[] speeds = {15, -10, 5, 10};
int turned = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < xs.length; i++) {
  xs[i] = xs[i] + speeds[i];
  if (xs[i] > 380 || xs[i] < 20) {
    speeds[i] = -speeds[i];
    turned++;
  }
}
System.out.println(turned + " " + xs[1] + " " + speeds[3]);
  1. 2 15 -10
  2. 3 15 -10
  3. 3 25 -10
  4. 3 15 10
  5. 4 15 -10

B  3 15 -10 Every session in one question. The moves give 385, 15, 205, and 405. Three of those are past a wall, so three speeds flip. A misses the first one, at 385. C forgets that xs[1] was updated before it was printed. D forgets that the flip changes the array. E counts 205 too.

Facilitator notes

Keep both extensions framed as options. Many schools restrict AI tools, and many classrooms have no hardware. The stand-in sketch exists so a hardware lesson never shuts out a student without a board.

Asynchronous route · about 60 minutes

On your own

The studio works with one colleague, live or not.

  1. Set up

    Download session-5-files.zip and choose one lesson package, yours or any session’s handout. About 5 minutes.

  2. Predict

    Answer questions 1 and 2 above in writing. About 5 minutes.

  3. Revise

    Work through the package checklist and missions 1–3. About 25 minutes.

  4. Try it

    Ask one colleague to do the first three minutes as a student, out loud. A video call works. Check your prediction. About 10 minutes.

  5. Critique

    Ask them only the two discussion prompt questions. Listen without responding. About 10 minutes.

  6. Change

    Make one change, and write one sentence about why. Share it where the cohort shares work. About 5 minutes.
